Can the AI compute boom pay for itself?

A bottom-up economic model of what customers might pay, how much revenue reaches AI model and GPU providers, and whether that cash flow can justify a historic infrastructure buildout.

The central finding
The economy can support a vast AI industry. That does not mean every GPU investment will earn its cost of capital.

Our base case generates about $551 billion of annual final-customer AI spending by 2030. Only $164 billion reaches GPU and AI-cloud infrastructure providers after accounting for the flow of payments between applications, models, and compute. At assumed operating margins, that is not enough on its own to recover repeated years of investment at today's pace.

What this model does—and does not—claim.

It is a transparent scenario analysis, not a forecast or a valuation. Published figures anchor the market context; adoption rates, value capture, compute mix, and economic lives are explicit assumptions.

Most importantly, downstream sales and upstream compute purchases are not additive sources of final demand.

01 / Demand

Start with what buyers can afford.

Rather than extrapolating GPU shipments into demand, derive AI spending from the value firms and consumers could realize.

For workplace AI, the relevant pool is economically useful work—not all world GDP. We assume $25 trillion in potentially addressable labor compensation. Only a fraction of tasks are exposed, only some exposed work improves, and only part of that uplift becomes measurable profit or output.

Workplace spending = $25T × exposure × uplift × realization × vendor capture

Under the base assumptions—70% task exposure, 20% productivity uplift on exposed tasks, 40% financial realization, and 25% vendor value capture—workplace spending reaches $350B/year. Consumer subscriptions and nonpayroll industrial uses add approximately $201B.

02 / Value capture

One AI dollar travels through three layers.

Application revenue, model revenue, and GPU revenue overlap. Counting them together exaggerates the size of the market.

Our illustrative distribution of every $100 paid by end users is $65 to application vendors, $25 directly to model vendors, and $10 directly to GPU/cloud vendors. Application companies then buy model APIs and compute; model providers also buy infrastructure.

This distribution assumes applications spend 25% of revenue on model APIs and another 5% directly on cloud GPUs. Model providers spend 40% of their revenue on compute. These are industry-mix assumptions; an individual company's economics may look very different.

Real data suggests compute is currently a large expense for frontier-model providers. Anthropic reported $16.286B in first-half 2026 revenue and $8.945B in compute and infrastructure expenses—about 55% of revenue. [6]

03 / Returns

The capital has to be recovered.

A spending boom is not proof of an investment return. Infrastructure owners need durable cash contribution after electricity, operations, maintenance and other costs.

We split a hypothetical $900B investment cohort into 60% shorter-lived compute equipment (five-year economic life) and 40% longer-lived facilities and supporting infrastructure (15-year life). At a 10% required return, the annual capital cash recovery target is about $190B while both asset classes are in recovery.

At a 55% infrastructure cash contribution margin, recovering this cohort exclusively through external compute rental would require approximately $345B of annual infrastructure revenue. Internal benefits, such as advertising monetization or defense of profitable platforms, may substitute for external cash revenue—but must be incremental and measurable.

If equivalent $900B cohorts were deployed annually for five years, their overlapping annual capital recovery requirement would approach $949B before additional investment. In the base case, recovery would then require approximately $5.5T of annual final-customer AI spending, holding the model's channel mix and margins fixed. That is an intentionally demanding stress test, not a prediction.

The unit-level GPU test

For a hypothetical accelerator with $60,000 fully installed capital cost, five-year recovery, 10% required return, 75% paid utilization, and a 50% cash contribution margin, the required realized rental price is approximately $4.82 per billed GPU-hour. Changing utilization or pricing materially changes the investment return.

What changes the result most?

Upside drivers

Agents turn saved hours into revenue or eliminated cost; usage expands into workflows with provable ROI; utilization remains high; hyperscalers monetize AI indirectly in advertising, search, commerce and productivity software.

Downside drivers

Inference prices compress faster than token consumption grows; customers prefer cheaper models; accelerator generations shorten useful lives; companies deploy AI without corresponding financial gains.

05 / Reality check

Adoption is real. Profit capture is less certain.

Three observations constrain the model without determining its outcome.

Observed indicatorReadingInterpretation
Enterprise AI spending~$300BBroad 2026 U.S. firm AI-related spending estimate; not equal to external GenAI sales [4]
AI-optimized IaaS$42.3B2026 global Gartner forecast for a narrower cloud infrastructure category [5]
Measured financial impact37%Share of McKinsey respondents attributing some enterprise-level EBIT impact to AI [7]

The numbers describe different scopes. They should not be summed or treated as measurements of the same market. They do suggest that actual spending is concentrated in infrastructure and that operational adoption has moved faster than reliably measured business profit.

My assessment: AI can become a very large and productive industry while capital owners still face disappointing returns. A technology being transformative and an investment being well-priced are two different propositions.
